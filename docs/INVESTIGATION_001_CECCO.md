# Investigation 001: The Bruni-Capponi Cecco Manuscript

Date opened: 26 July 2026  
Source: Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, `Cappon.283.pt.1`, ff. 117r-118r  
Status: active investigation, no discovery claim  
Claim ceiling: L2, research hypothesis

## Executive finding

A first reading suggested that Filippo Bruni's note merely described a
manuscript of Cecco d'Ascoli's known poem *L'Acerba*. A closer reading does not
support that simple dismissal.

Bruni's enclosed note quotes verses from *L'Acerba* and then states, in
substance:

> This work, however, called *L'Acerba*, is printed. The present one has never
> been seen; its meter, script, and paper prove it to be by Cecco.

The distinction between the printed *Acerba* and “the present” manuscript is
the central evidence. Bruni appears to have been claiming that a separate,
previously unknown work existed in 1745 and was attributable to Cecco.

This is not proof that:

- the manuscript was genuinely medieval;
- Cecco wrote it;
- it contained a separate work rather than an unusual or misunderstood
  *Acerba* witness;
- the text was unknown in 1745 or remains unknown today;
- the manuscript survives.

The defensible result is narrower: a dated 1745 document records an
unverified claim about a physically inspectable manuscript presented as an
unpublished work of Cecco d'Ascoli and explicitly distinguished from
*L'Acerba*.

## Source description

The Vatican catalog identifies ff. 117r-118r as a letter by Filippo Bruni
containing information about a manuscript with an unpublished work by Cecco
d'Ascoli. The accompanying cover letter is dated 4 May 1745 and addressed to
Marchese Alessandro Gregorio Capponi.

The surviving sequence appears to contain:

1. ff. 117r-117v: an unsigned descriptive note about the alleged manuscript;
2. f. 118r: Bruni's cover letter to Capponi;
3. f. 118v: blank, apart from show-through and later marks.

The described medieval manuscript itself is not visibly bound with these
folios.

are lightly normalized for readability. Square brackets and question marks

## Working translation

The note calls the manuscript an unpublished work by Cecco d'Ascoli, astrologer
to the Duke of Calabria. It advances an extremely early date and relies on
script, paper, meter, and literary chronology to attribute the manuscript to
Cecco. It then quotes the opening of a passage from *L'Acerba*, acknowledges
that *L'Acerba* had been printed, and distinguishes that known work from the
manuscript then under discussion.

Bruni's cover letter appears to say that he was sending or arranging to show
the manuscript to Capponi, together with the information that could be given
about it. This wording makes the manuscript sound like a physical object
available for inspection, not merely a rumor.

| The note distinguishes the manuscript from printed *L'Acerba* | Substantial | Explicit contrast on f. 117v |
| The quoted verses are from *L'Acerba* | Strong | Exact matches in modern and historical editions |
| The manuscript contained a separate Cecco work | Plausible | This is the note's apparent claim, not independently verified |
| Cecco wrote the manuscript or text | Weak | The note's paleographic reasoning is unsupported and chronologically suspect |
| The manuscript survives | Unknown | It is absent from the 1747 printed manuscript appendix under Cecco or *Acerba* |
| The text remains unknown to scholarship | Unknown | No shelfmark-specific scholarly discussion found in the first search pass |

## Reasons for skepticism

The note contains obvious historical problems. It gives Cecco's birth as 1248
and then appears to date the manuscript to about 1250, which would make the
claimed author a child. The date may be a misreading, a scribal error, or
evidence that the assessor's judgment was unreliable. Script and paper alone
could not establish authorship, and eighteenth-century scholars often treated
old-looking copies as authorial witnesses.

The quoted *Acerba* passages prove familiarity with Cecco's known poem. They do
not prove that the separate manuscript was by Cecco. The phrase “la presente
mai si è veduta” may mean unpublished, unknown to the writer, or simply unseen
by a particular circle.

## Provenance search

Alessandro Gregorio Capponi died in 1746 and bequeathed his manuscript
collection to the Vatican Library. The 1747 printed *Catalogo della Libreria
Capponi* lists 265 manuscript volumes. Its manuscript appendix contains no
searchable entry for Cecco, *L'Acerba*, or Ascoli.

Giuseppe Salvo Cozzo's 1897 catalog records Bruni's letter at Capponi 283,
f. 117, but does not identify a corresponding Cecco manuscript in the
collection. Cozzo also warns that manuscripts mentioned in Capponi's
correspondence did not always enter the Vatican collection. Since the famous
Capponi library fire occurred in 1734, it cannot explain the loss of an object
shown in 1745. Return to its owner, sale, dispersal after Capponi's death, or
later recataloging are more plausible possibilities.

## Hypotheses to test

1. **Separate lost or unidentified work.** The manuscript contained a work
   distinct from *L'Acerba* and was wrongly or rightly attributed to Cecco.
2. **Misidentified *Acerba* witness.** The assessor mistook an unusual,
   incomplete, reorganized, or untitled copy of *L'Acerba* for another work.
3. **Composite manuscript.** A known *Acerba* witness was bound with another
   text, and the note conflated the parts.
4. **Early modern fabrication or antiquarian error.** The manuscript was much
   later than claimed and imitated Cecco's meter or language.
5. **Object returned or dispersed.** Bruni showed the manuscript to Capponi,
   but Capponi did not acquire it or it left the collection before the Vatican
   transfer of December 1746.

## Falsification plan

The separate-work hypothesis should be rejected or revised if:

- a full expert transcription shows that “la presente” refers to the same
  *Acerba* manuscript rather than a different text;
- a known *Acerba* witness matches every physical and textual clue;
- the manuscript can be identified in a later catalog under a known title;
- Bruni's attribution is shown to repeat an already documented antiquarian
  error.

It would be materially strengthened if:

- another letter names the owner, title, incipit, dimensions, folio count, or
  provenance;
- a sale or library catalog lists a matching manuscript after May 1745;
- a surviving anonymous poem in Cecco-like terzines carries matching provenance;
- a specialist confirms that the wording unambiguously distinguishes two
  physical or textual works.

## Next research actions

1. Obtain two independent specialist transcriptions of ff. 117r-118r.
2. Search the surrounding 1745 correspondence for Bruni, the unnamed owner,
   later purchase, return, payment, or description.
3. Identify Filippo Bruni precisely. A contemporary Piarist and later reviewer
   of Vatican publications is a possible match, not yet established.
4. Build a census of known *Acerba* manuscripts, incipits, explicit forms,
   chapter structures, and eighteenth-century provenances.
5. Search Roman auction, bookseller, family, Ottoboni, and Capponi records from
   1745-1800 for an anonymous early Italian poem in terzines.
6. Ask a Cecco d'Ascoli specialist and an Italian paleographer to review the
   dossier before any novelty claim.
