# High-Impact Search Strategy

## Objective

Project Lumen does not maximize the number of translated pages. It maximizes the
expected historical value of verified findings.

The basic prioritization formula is:

`expected value = impact ceiling × unresolved probability × evidentiary tractability × verification feasibility`

A dramatic theory with little testable evidence should rank below a less
sensational question that could produce a durable correction to historical
knowledge.

## Grand-prize discovery classes

### 1. Recovering a lost or overwritten text

Potential contribution:

- a work known only by title or quotation;
- a missing section of an influential work;
- an erased earlier text beneath a later manuscript;
- a fragment that can be joined to material held elsewhere.

Why it matters:

This can return a primary source to history rather than merely reinterpret an
existing one.

Search signals:

- palimpsest, rescript, reused parchment, fragment, binding waste;
- unusual undertext or offset writing;
- discontinuities in script, ink, language, or page construction;
- catalog descriptions mentioning illegibility or unidentified fragments.

### 2. Correcting the authorship of an influential text

Potential contribution:

- identifying the author, scribe, drafter, or editor of an anonymous work;
- distinguishing a famous person's own words from a secretary's;
- showing that a text came from a workshop, office, or intellectual circle.

Why it matters:

Authorship changes how historians interpret intention, authority, influence,
and responsibility.

Required evidence:

- dated comparison samples;
- handwriting or scribal-feature analysis;
- stylometry suitable to the language and genre;
- provenance, chronology, and network compatibility;
- serious unknown-author alternatives.

### 3. Changing the chronology of a major event or idea

Potential contribution:

- an idea appearing earlier than currently documented;
- a draft proving that a policy or doctrine developed in a different sequence;
- a report demonstrating that an institution knew something earlier or later
  than accepted.

Why it matters:

Chronology affects causation. Moving a date can overturn explanations of who
influenced whom or why a decision occurred.

Search signals:

- dated letters and drafts;
- watermarks, calendars, regnal years, feast days, and astronomical references;
- references to identifiable events;
- corrections to dates or names.

### 4. Revealing unknown evidence behind a consequential decision

Potential contribution:

- advice received before a major political, religious, legal, diplomatic, or
  scientific decision;
- evidence omitted from the final record;
- a draft showing meaningful changes in language or intent;
- a previously unknown intermediary.

Why it matters:

Such evidence can alter the accepted causes, motives, or responsibility for an
important decision.

Search signals:

- drafts, memoranda, minutes, dispatches, petitions, private letters;
- multiple versions of the same document;
- canceled paragraphs and interlinear additions;
- recipient annotations and routing marks.

### 5. Finding a materially different version of a major text

Potential contribution:

- an early version with substantial differences;
- a translation preserving lost wording;
- a variant that changes doctrine, law, science, politics, or literature;
- evidence of deliberate revision or censorship.

Why it matters:

Variants reveal how ideas changed and which version influenced later history.

Required comparison:

- critical editions;
- stemmata or manuscript-family analysis;
- dated witnesses;
- quotations in other works;
- translation direction and dependence.

### 6. Reconstructing an unknown high-level network

Potential contribution:

- previously invisible communication among major actors;
- a broker connecting courts, scholars, religious institutions, merchants, or
  diplomats;
- the route through which a decisive text, technology, or report traveled.

Why it matters:

History often changes through networks rather than isolated individuals.

Search signals:

- repeated messengers, secretaries, translators, and copyists;
- address formulas, endorsements, seals, and docketing;
- clusters of names, dates, and locations;
- copies of the same passage in distant collections.

### 7. Identifying a major source used by an influential thinker

Potential contribution:

- a manuscript annotation showing direct reading;
- copied language connecting a famous work to an unknown source;
- ownership or lending evidence;
- a translation or intermediary explaining transmission.

Why it matters:

This can change intellectual genealogy and the history of influence.

### 8. Correcting the provenance of a major object or manuscript

Potential contribution:

- earlier ownership;
- a different place of production;
- evidence of theft, sale, gift, diplomatic transfer, or wartime movement;
- identification of separated manuscript parts.

Why it matters:

Provenance affects dating, authenticity, legal history, cultural ownership, and
interpretation.

## Priority order for the Grand Survey

1. Search metadata across all 30,900 currently listed digitized manuscripts.
2. Flag records with uncertain attribution, unidentified contents, fragments,
   sparse bibliography, drafts, multiple hands, marginalia, erasures, or unusual
   provenance.
3. Cross-reference the flagged records with historically important people,
   works, events, and unresolved scholarly debates.
4. Rank by impact ceiling and ability to verify.
5. Inspect the top 500 at image level.
6. Build dossiers for the top 50.
7. Ask relevant specialists to help select the first three investigations.

## Automatic exclusion or penalty

Reduce priority when:

- the manuscript is famous and supported by a mature critical edition;
- the proposed contribution is merely a modern-language translation;
- the claim depends on symbolism that cannot be independently tested;
- there is no qualified reviewer for the script or field;
- access or rights conditions prevent responsible study;
- the historical importance comes only from public fascination;
- the question is broad enough that almost any reading could appear to support
  it.

## First concrete operation

The first real research task is a metadata census of these four high-ceiling
collection families:

1. Vaticani latini;
2. Barberiniani latini;
3. Chigiani;
4. Archivio del Capitolo di San Pietro.

The census should not read every page. It should locate the small fraction of
items with both a potentially major historical question and enough independent
evidence to test it.
